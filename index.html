What it was like, what I did, and what it has not fixed

In August the academic conversation was not written down anywhere. Of 14,403 helpdesk cases, 45 were a child not progressing, and what was tried, by whom and whether it worked lived in heads and DMs. A guide asking why a student was stuck waited on a hand-built deep dive: 77 were requested in 37 days, 70 are still open, the oldest 30 days.

Ninety days on, the function runs on a record and the record is public. 1,666 interventions logged across 86 campuses, 15 of 16 DRIs filing, half closed inside 14.6 hours. Other teams read this function through a data contract I wrote. Every figure on this page is produced by a pull and re-derived by a second route — 138 checks, 0 failing — and the build refuses to run against a failing audit.

What none of it has yet moved is the number it exists to move. Accuracy for the same 289 children held flat across the year — 86.1% to 86.2% — and more of them declined than improved (154 against 135, p = 0.29, not significant on a sign test). Language and Math are each down 2.8. This is work done and work made visible, not yet a child who learned more because of it: the log is 37 days old, so there is no before to compare against. The measure I would accept being held to, and the date it can first be read, are on The evidence.

Why me

Six claims. Each one is a number on another tab, not an adjective — so each can be checked, and each can be argued with.

1

The team already runs on what I built.

The intervention log was not rolled out to anyone. It was built, and the team moved onto it because it was the only place the work could be written down. That is the strongest evidence I can offer that I already do this job: the practice the whole function follows is one I designed, and it stuck without a mandate.

Evidence: 15 of the 16 Campus DRIs file into it, alongside 4 Curriculum DRIs — 21 people writing, 10 closing, across 86 campuses, since 2026-09-04.

2

I set the standard other teams read us through.

alpha_dri_interventions is published to the data-source-skill contract with a dictionary, query rules, and a hand-written judgement layer saying what the values mean — which interventions signal a platform failure, which are routine, and what good looks like. When another team asks how the DRI function is performing, they get the answer through a definition I wrote. A Head of DRIs has to own what the role means to the rest of the org; I already do.

Evidence: Source owner and key issuer — credentials are minted per person, by me. The rules bind hard enough that this page obeys them: the two logs are never totalled, a row is reported as a touch rather than a failure, and the two minutes columns are scoped separately. A check over every row found zero violations.

3

I already carry near-top scope, while building for everyone else.

The caseload is not evenly shared, and I am at the heavy end of it — while also being the person maintaining the platform the other fifteen monitor through. Doing both is the closest thing to a trial run for the role.

Evidence: 1,249 students across 8 campuses, 3rd of 16 — 13% of the network. On the platform itself, 1,291 of 2,657 commits (49%).

4

I made the role measurable — including my own work.

Before the log there was no record of what was tried for a stalled student, by whom, or whether it worked. That made the function unarguable in both directions: no credit for the work, and no way to find what was not working. A Head cannot manage this function on anecdote. I built the evidence layer the job needs, and I am inside it like everyone else.

Evidence: 1,666 touches over 788 students in 37 days, median close 14.6 hours, 69% inside a day, and 370 hours of effort now attributable to a named person. For scale: of all 14,403 helpdesk cases, only 45 are a stuck child — the academic conversation was never in the ticket queue, and now it has somewhere to live.

5

I asked the guides, and the hardest fixes were people, not tooling.

Before the year I surveyed the guides the role exists to serve — 60 responses from 41 campuses. 10 themes of criticism came back, and only 4 of them were answered by building something. 3 were answered by changing how the role is coached and 3 by a decision about a person. 2 of the 10 were one Campus DRI — the same person behind both the complaint about message volume and the one about blaming guides for content they could not fix. They were moved off the campus, the feedback was handed to their replacement, and the original DRI was coached. They are doing the job well now. That is the half of this role no system solves, and it is the half the job is actually for. One theme I told the guides they were wrong about: the skill-plan delay they were putting on DRI responsiveness comes from the automation that generates the plans, so no amount of clearing a DRI queue would have moved it. They were chasing the wrong team, and saying so was part of the answer.

Evidence: Every theme, in the guides' own words, with what was done about it, on the Guide feedback tab — 7 done, 2 partly, 1 still in progress, and each one labelled with whether it was answered by tooling, by coaching, or by a personnel decision.

6

Missing data no longer reads as a child doing nothing.

Mid-week reports were putting working students in the RED tier at 0.0 XP/day. On one campus report, seven of the nine children listed under little to no engagement had earned XP every single day — one read 0.0 against 118 XP actually earned. The tell was that their time was right to the minute while only the XP had gone: the two are fetched from different places, so the loss was in the cache build, not in the report. Underneath it, a paged fetch ended on any non-200 and returned whatever it already had. The API answers 503 under load, and that is as likely on the first page as any other, so what came back was usually nothing at all — with no exception and no flag, and a student with no XP days is tiered RED automatically. An outage read as a child doing no work, and a DRI acting on that report spends the week on a child who never needed it while the ones who did go unseen.

Evidence: Fixed as a class rather than as an incident: a page that cannot be fetched now raises instead of truncating, the failure is recorded per student, and the report holds those children out of the tiers entirely under DATA UNAVAILABLE — because a child whose data would not load is not evidence that they did nothing. It took three commits in four days, since the first covered only the deep dive's path and the guard in the second stopped firing exactly when it was needed most, once a fallback began supplying the name it keyed on. The pulls behind this page refuse to write a short file for the same reason, and every figure on it is re-derived by a second route: 138 checks, 0 failing.

What I would do with the job

Four problems visible in the team's own data. None of these are guesses; each is a number on another tab, and each is why the role needs someone who reads them.

ProblemWhat the data showsWhat I would do
The caseload is lopsided The top three DRIs carry 42% of all students between them, and the largest caseload is 9.9× the smallest. Rebalance against live enrolment rather than campus count. A DRI with two campuses can be carrying more children than one with ten.
Logging is not yet uniform Filings range from 352 to 2 across the 15 DRIs who file at all. 1 of 16 have filed nothing in 37 days, between them responsible for 1,337 students — including the 2nd-largest caseload on the team. That spread is either real workload difference or inconsistent practice, and right now nobody can tell which. Make the log a standard, then use it to spot who needs support — not to rank people.
Requests outrun the answer 77 deep dives asked for in 37 days; the hand-built route closed 1. 70 are still open, the oldest 30 days. This is the queue the bot exists for. Finish the rollout, measure it, and hold the answer time to the same 14.6-hour standard the rest of the log meets.
The data under us is not clean The email allowlist disagrees with live enrolment on 60 campuses — GT Anywhere reads 510 against 1,259 enrolled. A team measured on student outcomes cannot run on a roster that is wrong by a factor of two. Reconcile to enrolment, and keep it reconciled.

The honest caveats

Stated here rather than buried, because a case a reviewer can puncture is worth less than one that punctures itself first.

I am not the heaviest user of my own log 241 filings puts me 4th of 15, not first. The claim is that the team adopted it, not that I use it most.
This is outputs, not outcomes Everything here measures work done and work made visible. It does not yet show a child who learned more because of it — the log is 37 days old and there is no before to compare against, because nothing was recorded before it. What closes that gap: the log is itself the missing baseline. Every row since 2026-09-04 records the trigger and the date, so by 9 January 2027 there is a before and an after for the 788 students already in it. The measure I would accept being held to, in the job: accuracy and XP in the 30 days after an intervention against the 30 days before, reported by intervention type — with the types that move nobody named as plainly as the ones that work. A remediation that does not change a number is one the team should stop spending time on, and right now nothing would tell us.
The window is short 37 days. Long enough to show a rate, not long enough to show a trend.
The platform is not solo work 49% of commits are mine, which means 51% are not. On the intervention tab specifically it is 42 of 63.

At 8,514 rostered students across 106 campuses that question simply does not get asked.

The Slack Bot

Why it has to exist

The dashboard answers questions you already know to ask, in a browser, at a desk. Real questions arrive in Slack, from a guide, mid-lesson: “why is this student stuck?” The demand is measured, not assumed — in 37 days 77 deep dives were requested by hand for 53 students, and the hand-built path closed 1. 70 are still open, the oldest waiting 30 days — and the queue has grown, not shrunk, since this page was first built. That queue is the argument for the bot.

A Slack bot that turns a plain-English question into a rendered report in the thread. The model picks one tool and writes a single framing sentence — it never restates a figure, because the tool result is rendered directly. That division is deliberate: a number cannot drift between the API and the channel, and an uncertain request comes back as a clarifying question instead of a confident answer about the wrong student.

The one that closes the guide feedback loop

interventions

The newest tool, and the one that answers the loudest complaint in the guide survey — “it's been escalated to the appropriate team, with no follow up” and “guides have to escalate 10+ times before anything is done”. A guide can now ask what happened to the thing they raised and get the answer themselves: which interventions exist for a student, who owns each one, and whether it is still open. The status became something a guide can pull rather than something they have to chase.

How it holds up

3-second ack Slack kills any request that has not responded within three seconds, so the route verifies, hands the work to a background task, and returns immediately. Real answers are posted back into the thread afterwards.
De-duplicated on event_id Slack redelivers on any non-200 and occasionally duplicates outright. Answering the same question three times in a thread is worse than answering it late.
The model never retypes a figure It picks the tool and writes one framing sentence. The tool returns structured data and the renderer lays it out, so identical data produces an identical message and a number cannot drift between the API and the channel.
It asks rather than guesses No email and none in the thread, or a campus that could be one of several — it comes back with a question. Answering the wrong student's deep dive confidently is worse than asking which campus was meant.
It reads the thread An email given in the first message and a date given in the fifth are both available; the facts are pulled out with a regular expression rather than left to the model to remember, because it kept re-asking for what it had already been told.

The Resource Estate

Why it has to exist

The same study guide gets rebuilt at five campuses by five people who do not know the other four exist. Every hour spent regenerating an artefact that already exists is an hour not spent on a student who is stuck — and when the artefact is a reading passage, rebuilding it badly is worse than not having it.

Three things, built for three different problems: a shared library so a resource anyone adds is a resource everyone has, a standalone reading site because the texts guides needed were locked inside a vendor console one lesson at a time, and a Student Resources tab on the academic dashboard so the material a guide hands a child is not a private bookmark.

Time to resolution — the distribution, not the median

The shape matters more than any single number: 24% of cases close inside an hour by automation and most of the rest take three days to two weeks, so the overall median (6.4 days) falls in the empty valley between the two humps and describes almost nothing. For the cases a human actually worked, the median is 6.7 days.

6.7 d → 14.6 h

A problem raised through the general helpdesk waits a median of 6.7 days for a human resolution. An academic stall raised through the purpose-built intervention loop is closed in 14.6 hours. These are different kinds of work and the comparison is not apples to apples — but it is the same organisation, the same period and largely the same students, and it is the clearest available evidence for what a dedicated loop buys over a general queue.

Method & provenance

Method & provenance

  • Accuracy — one API call per student over the whole Alpha roster (2,037 resolved, 1,368 with recorded activity in the window), 2026-08-10 to 2026-09-17, pulled 2026-09-18. Cohort key is the student's own OneRoster grade, not a per-subject working grade. Figures are pooled (Σ correct ÷ Σ answered), never an average of per-student averages. The 42 is the number of distinct apps across the whole roster, not per student — a typical student is in 7 and the most any one student uses is 14. Those “apps” are the distinct source names the API reports, which includes a few internal surfaces (TimeBack Dash, manual XP assignment) alongside the learning apps proper; the by-app chart shows the 16 largest by volume, all real apps.
  • Interventions — read from the alpha_dri_interventions source, scoped to its published rules: the two section values are independent logs and are never totalled; a row is a touch, so folded case counts are given alongside; minutes are read from minutes_spent for Campus DRI manual work and completion_minutes for Curriculum DRI answers, which the store keeps disjoint by constraint.
  • Turnaround — completed_at − created_at over completed Campus DRI rows. All 1177 qualify. The 251 Curriculum DRI rows are excluded because that log is written retrospectively, which would otherwise produce negative elapsed times.
  • Support tickets — the Kayako instance carries 40,593 cases across ~219 brands and its API accepts no brand filter, so the pull reads every case and filters locally to the 14,403 Alpha-brand ones. Resolution is measured to last_completed_at, the agent's resolving reply — not last_closed_at, an auto-close firing days later that reads 72 hours against a real resolution of 0.1. The pull's page cap was raised from 40,000 to 45,000 for this run: the instance had outgrown it and the previous run truncated silently at exactly 40,000 without reporting anything.
  • Ticket categories — ordered rules over the subject line, first match wins, each case recording the rule that caught it. The preview text is only consulted when the subject is under twelve characters, because the preview is the last message on a case and is usually the agent's closing reply: matching on it classifies a ticket by how it ended rather than what it was. Rules that match only a bare topic word are counted separately and never folded into a named category.
  • Repository figures — counted live from the working copies at build time with git log and grep, not recalled.
