Why promote me

Five claims. Each one is a number on another tab, not an adjective — so each one can be checked, and each one can be argued with.

1

I own the layer the role runs on.

The TimeBack dashboard is the platform every Campus DRI monitors students through. It is not a side project I contributed to; it is the thing I have been building, and the playbook's monitoring routine was written on top of it because the tool and the process were designed together.

Evidence: 1,179 of 2,424 commits (49%), 106 campuses, 8,606 rostered students, 136 API endpoints, 23 report generators.

2

I build what is missing, not what is asked for.

Nobody filed a ticket asking for a Slack bot. The queue asked for it: deep dives were being requested faster than anyone could hand-build them, and a request that waits ten days is a student who went ten days without the answer. I read the backlog as a specification.

Evidence: 42 deep dives requested by hand in 18 days for 29 students; the manual path closed 1, 35 are still open, the oldest waiting 10 days.

3

I made the work measurable — including my own.

Before the intervention log there was no record of what was tried for a stalled student, by whom, or whether it worked. That made the whole function unarguable in both directions: no credit for the work, and no way to find what was not working. It is now the only record of remediation the platform did not do by itself.

Evidence: 613 touches over 355 students in 18 days, median close 17.2 hours, 66% inside a day, and 160 hours of effort now attributable to a named person. For scale: classifying all 14,403 helpdesk cases finds 45 that are a stuck child needing a plan. The log captured 613 in 18 days — the work was always happening, it simply was not written down anywhere.

4

I own a data source other teams read through.

alpha_dri_interventions is published to the data-source-skill contract with a dictionary, query rules and a hand-written judgement layer saying what the values mean — which interventions signal a platform failure, which are routine, and what good looks like. Credentials are issued per person, by me.

Evidence: Source owner and key issuer. The rules are binding enough that this page obeys them: the two logs are never totalled, a row is reported as a touch rather than a failure, and the two minutes columns are scoped separately. A check over every row found zero violations.

5

I find what is failing silently.

The failures that matter are the ones nothing reports. Mid-week reports were putting working students in the RED tier at 0.0 XP/day — on one report, seven of the nine students listed as having little to no engagement had earned XP every single day. The cause was a fetch that returned empty instead of raising when the API answered 503, so an outage read as a child doing no work. I fixed the fetch and added a guard so an unloadable student is shown as unknown rather than as a zero.

Evidence: 547 of my 1,179 commits are fixes (317 are features). The same instinct caught a silent cap in the helpdesk pull behind this page — it stopped at exactly 40,000 cases against an instance of 40,593 and reported nothing.

One loop, built end to end

These are not four tools that happen to sit near each other. They are four stages of one loop, and each exists because the stage before it produced something nobody could act on.

The honest caveats

Stated here rather than buried, because a case a reviewer can puncture is worth less than one that punctures itself first.

The helpdesk comparison 6.7 days against 17.2 hours is not like for like — a support ticket and an academic stall are different work. It is the same organisation, period and largely the same students, which is why it is worth showing, but it is not a controlled comparison.
The intervention window is short 18 days. Long enough to show a rate, not long enough to show a trend.
Effort capture is partial The form only began asking on 2026-09-11, so 239 of 311 eligible requests since then carry minutes (77%), and anything completed earlier carries none.
The dashboard is not solo work 49% of commits are mine, which means 51% are not. On the intervention tab specifically it is 35 of 55.

The Deep Dive Bot

Why it has to exist

The dashboard answers questions you already know to ask, in a browser, at a desk. Real questions arrive in Slack, from a guide, mid-lesson: “why is this student stuck?” The demand is measured, not assumed — in 18 days 42 deep dives were requested by hand for 29 students, and the hand-built path closed 1. 35 are still open, the oldest waiting 10 days. That queue is the argument for the bot.

A Slack bot that turns a plain-English question into a rendered report in the thread. The model picks one tool and writes a single framing sentence — it never restates a figure, because the tool result is rendered directly. That division is deliberate: a number cannot drift between the API and the channel, and an uncertain request comes back as a clarifying question instead of a confident answer about the wrong student.

The Intervention Log

Why it has to exist

Measuring a stall and doing nothing about it is just a report. When TimeBack does not resolve a student's stall by itself somebody has to intervene — and before this there was no record of what was tried, by whom, or whether it worked. It is the only record of remediation the platform did not do by itself, and the only place the trigger for each one is written down.

Two independent logs: requests a Campus DRI raises and pushes to a Curriculum DRI, and work the Campus DRI absorbs themselves.

The Resource Library

Why it has to exist

The same study guide gets rebuilt at five campuses by five people who do not know the other four exist. Every hour spent regenerating an artefact that already exists is an hour not spent on a student who is stuck.

A shared library on its own Railway service with Postgres behind it, so a resource added by anyone appears for everyone rather than living in one person's browser. Two roles — staff see everything, students never receive staff-only rows — plus generated study-guide and practice-test pages.

What the helpdesk is actually made of

14,403 cases on the Alpha / 2hr Learning brands of the Trilogy central helpdesk, pulled from the Kayako API on 2026-09-22. Sorted into four kinds of work plus an honest fifth for the ones whose subject line never says what the ask is.

The single most useful number here. Of 14,403 support cases, 45 are a child who is stuck and needs a plan — a deep dive, a custom plan, an accuracy investigation. The helpdesk is where the platform gets fixed and where accounts get made. It is not where the academic conversation happens. That work had nowhere to live, which is exactly why the intervention log had to be built: it captured 613 of those in 18 days, more in under three weeks than the helpdesk saw all year.

How the work behaves over time

Time to resolution — the distribution, not the median

The shape matters more than any single number: 24% of cases close inside an hour by automation and most of the rest take three days to two weeks, so the overall median (6.4 days) falls in the empty valley between the two humps and describes almost nothing. For the cases a human actually worked, the median is 6.7 days.

6.7 d → 17.2 h

A problem raised through the general helpdesk waits a median of 6.7 days for a human resolution. An academic stall raised through the purpose-built intervention loop is closed in 17.2 hours. These are different kinds of work and the comparison is not apples to apples — but it is the same organisation, the same period and largely the same students, and it is the clearest available evidence for what a dedicated loop buys over a general queue.

How honest this classification is

Rules over the subject line, evaluated in a fixed order, every case recording the rule that caught it. 73.2% of cases land in a named category on a rule that matches a verb or a failure signature. The other 4,125 are held in Needs triage rather than forced somewhere flattering.

The inherited four-bucket taxonomy reported 76% coverage and 2,006 “Academic — not learning” cases. Reading its own rule labels, 1,189 of those 2,006 were caught by a bare subject word — “reading”, “math”, “language” — with no verb attached, and the samples are mostly platform work: “TimeBack PowerPath placement 500s”, “Fix Eitan Barkai reading plan assignment”. A helpdesk that is overwhelmingly platform and administration came to look academic on the strength of the word “reading”. This version reports a lower coverage number and a truer one.

Method & provenance

Method & provenance

  • Accuracy — one API call per student over the whole Alpha roster (2,037 resolved, 1,368 with recorded activity in the window), 2026-08-10 to 2026-09-17, pulled 2026-09-18. Cohort key is the student's own OneRoster grade, not a per-subject working grade. Figures are pooled (Σ correct ÷ Σ answered), never an average of per-student averages. The 42 “apps” are the distinct source names the API reports, which includes a few internal surfaces (TimeBack Dash, manual XP assignment) alongside the learning apps proper; the by-app chart shows the 16 largest by volume, all real apps.
  • Interventions — read from the alpha_dri_interventions source, scoped to its published rules: the two section values are independent logs and are never totalled; a row is a touch, so folded case counts are given alongside; minutes are read from minutes_spent for Campus DRI manual work and completion_minutes for Curriculum DRI answers, which the store keeps disjoint by constraint.
  • Turnaround — completed_at − created_at over completed Campus DRI rows. All 414 qualify. The 173 Curriculum DRI rows are excluded because that log is written retrospectively, which would otherwise produce negative elapsed times.
  • Support tickets — the Kayako instance carries 40,593 cases across ~219 brands and its API accepts no brand filter, so the pull reads every case and filters locally to the 14,403 Alpha-brand ones. Resolution is measured to last_completed_at, the agent's resolving reply — not last_closed_at, an auto-close firing days later that reads 72 hours against a real resolution of 0.1. The pull's page cap was raised from 40,000 to 45,000 for this run: the instance had outgrown it and the previous run truncated silently at exactly 40,000 without reporting anything.
  • Ticket categories — ordered rules over the subject line, first match wins, each case recording the rule that caught it. The preview text is only consulted when the subject is under twelve characters, because the preview is the last message on a case and is usually the agent's closing reply: matching on it classifies a ticket by how it ended rather than what it was. Rules that match only a bare topic word are counted separately and never folded into a named category.
  • Repository figures — counted live from the working copies at build time with git log and grep, not recalled.

Rebuild the whole page with python3 build_systems.py. The three pull scripts that produce its inputs are in this repository; no figure on any tab is typed by hand.
